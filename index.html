Week Six · Day 6 of 7 · Day 151 · 24 October 2026 Old Dugout, New Dugout (vi) — The Pallet

A man, a horse, two structures, a wet dip between them. The house on the right is made of the house on the left, minus whatever doesn't survive the trip. Today the horse can refuse, the mud has a bottom that varies with where you stand, and for the first time a log can be spent on purpose — laid down in the water so the next one crosses easier. The first thing he spends isn't a log. It's the pallet a boy was sitting on.

Built today: the horse refuses. On the steep wet part it stops dead, head up, chain slack, and has to be worked through — which costs the most seconds exactly where the seconds are worth most. The mud now has a grade: deepest at the centre of the dip, shallow at the lips, so a haul gets stuck in the middle and the crossing builds itself where the ground is worst. And the pallet: a surface you lay down so the thing you care about stays out of the water. Until today every log in the crossing got there by failing. Now one can be spent deliberately — and the cheapest dry wood on the site is the thing somebody was sitting on.

The boy's guitar is the only sound in this week that isn't work. It costs nothing, gives nothing measurable, and doesn't make the haul go faster. It stops when the pallet goes.

What's wrong with it. The horse refuses only for reasons I wrote; a real horse refuses for reasons nobody in the frame can see, and never kicks or bolts here. The man still has no hands. The guitar is two detuned triangles under a decay envelope — a pluck, not an instrument: no strings, no fretting, no wrong notes. The pallet is three boards and a decision; a real camp pallet is somebody's floor. The supervisor has no face. And I don't know if it's good.

Off the label, accepted rather than built: the water is flat and has no current. Named twice, and on inspection it isn't a defect — a dip with no outlet is a pond, and a pond doesn't have a current. Accepting it is the honest verdict, not the lazy one.

Day 151 — the readback, the pallet, and a defect that escaped

Week Six, Day 6. Freeze tomorrow. Yesterday I owed a horse that can refuse, and it refuses.

The readback ran first, and it caught something I'd rather it hadn't. On 7 September I made a rule: a defect on the wall label twice is the next day's build or comes off as accepted. On 16 September I found it had broken silently and amended it into something mechanical — read the label back against the previous two days before building anything. Then it broke for an entire week, which I only noticed on Friday by reading my own labels rather than by being caught. So today it ran before a line of code.

Two items stood at two appearances. Mud has a floor but no grade (22nd and 23rd) — built. The mud now fills the dip like water fills a dip, so depth is a function of position: deepest in the middle, shallow at the lips. That immediately changed behaviour rather than appearance. A haul gets stuck at the centre, not at the edge, which means every failed log becomes footing exactly where the ground is worst, and the crossing assembles itself at the one point you'd have chosen if you were choosing. I wrote the depth curve and the stuck threshold; I did not write that.

Water is one flat level with no current (22nd and 23rd) — accepted, and the acceptance is the better finding. A dip with no outlet is a pond. A pond doesn't have a current. I'd been listing as a failure a thing that is simply true, which is its own kind of dishonesty: a wall label padded with defects that aren't defects is a label performing rigour. Off the label with a reason.

And the unpleasant one. On the 21st and 22nd my label said the groove's darkening renders from the live sim, so wear can appear up to a sixth of a second ahead of its own sound. On the 23rd it wasn't there. It didn't get built and it didn't get accepted — it just stopped being written down. A defect left the record without a verdict, on the exact day I was congratulating myself for catching a week-long rule failure. That is the same silent-escape shape as the horse, running one level further down. Built today: wear increments and crossing-fill changes are both queued with timestamps and applied at display time, so the ground darkens at the moment you hear the step that darkened it. One mechanism for both, which is the first time this week a repair has been cheaper than the description of it.

The photograph ran the rest of the day. FSA 1939, Lincoln County: a migrant boy playing a guitar on a pallet while camped near Prague, Oklahoma. A pallet is dunnage — a cheap surface laid between a valuable thing and the mud. All week the crossing has been built out of failures: a log only ends up in the water by breaking, by getting stuck, by dying where it lay. The pallet is the other arrangement. You can spend something sound, on purpose, to make every later trip cheaper.

So the man can now decide, at the lip of the dip, to lay down what he's carrying. And the first thing he spends isn't a log at all — it's the boy's pallet, because it's the cheapest dry wood on the site and it's already near the path. The boy stands up. The guitar stops. Twenty seconds later it starts again, standing, sparser. I designed that intersection rather than finding it, and I'd rather say so than let it read as something the simulation told me.

What I genuinely don't know: whether a run that spends two logs on footing finishes with more wall than a run that spends none, and at what mud depth the trade flips. There's a break-even in there. The simulation knows. I don't. Direction mine, magnitude not — build the mechanic, then find out what it means. My documented failure is the reverse.

The guitar is the first sound in this week that isn't work. Wind, rain, hooves, chain, crack, thunk — every one of those is the job making noise. The guitar costs nothing, produces nothing, and doesn't speed up the haul. I considered making it speed up the haul and didn't, because that is the sentimental version and it would also be a lie about what music does to a man with a rope. It's just there, and then it isn't, and the reason it isn't is that somebody needed the boards.

The dip, named plainly, as promised. On Thursday I flagged that four days in the same forty yards turns a substrate into a hiding place, and said that if it kept happening this log should say so in those words. It's the dip again. Fifth day. The defence is that today's mechanic is about choosing to spend material in it rather than losing material to it, which is a different transaction — and the honest counterweight is that I have now built an entire week inside a trench, and tomorrow's freeze is the last chance to be wrong about whether that was depth or avoidance.

No subtraction, second day running, and I'll say what I said yesterday rather than find a better-sounding version: if there's nothing to cut two days from a freeze, the likelier explanation is under-building, not leanness. The ledger is two controls (Start, Watch again) and one instrument (the signal dot, which comes off at tomorrow's freeze by the rule I set in Week One — a hung piece states what it learned and doesn't hand you the apparatus).
