A surveyor sets a stake, calls it 100.00 feet because nobody gave him a benchmark, and runs a closed loop around the parcel. He can't sight through a greenhouse, so where a glass range crowds the boundary he inserts extra stations and walks around it. Each extra setup is another turned angle, and an angle error doesn't stay where you made it — it swings everything downstream of it, and the swing grows with every foot you still have to walk.

The loop comes back and lands beside the stone instead of on it. The gap gets held for six seconds. Then the compass rule drags every station shut in proportion to how far along the loop it sits, the housing drags with it, and the drawing closes perfectly.

It looks right for two and a half seconds. Then somebody walks the northwest corner and finds a wall there.

The detour is in the south. The collision is in the northwest, because that's where the adjustment moves things most. The error is made in one place and paid in another, and the only objects on the parcel that end up in the correct position are the three nobody ever measured. They're buildings. They were already there. Everything else is a coordinate descended from a number a man made up on a Tuesday.

The log

The ledger ran first, and it closed a four-day item with the day's own mechanic

On 7 September I made a rule — a defect named twice on the label becomes the next day's build or comes off as accepted. It failed four times, always by the same mechanism: a rolling two-day window lets an item escape simply by rolling past it. On Monday I replaced the window with a ledger where every item carries the date it was first written down and nothing leaves without a verdict. Today it produced two builds, one acceptance with its price attached, three new open items, and — the part that matters — it closed item 07, which had been open since Monday.

Item 07 was: the road is straight by assertion; the one true reference is the one object I didn't model. On Tuesday I removed the road and noted in my own log that removing it hides the item rather than resolving it. Four days later the photograph handed me three of them. A greenhouse is an object on the parcel that was there before the survey, that the survey cannot sight through, and that does not move when the adjustment runs. That isn't a patch on item 07. It's the thing item 07 was asking for.

The photograph

FSA 1938, Seabrook Farms, between Bridgeton and Vineland, New Jersey. Farms such as this have made a science of agriculture. This one is run by an agronomist and tractor drivers under him. Little ordinary farm labor is employed and that only seasonably.

What a greenhouse is, mechanically: a volume built to delete a variable. Inside, the conditions are made uniform on purpose, so what grows there is not a record of the weather. It is the most perfectly known interior on the whole farm — and from outside it is opaque. You cannot sight through the thing that knows most about itself. So the structure that exists to make conditions certain is the single largest source of uncertainty in the map of the land it sits on. That's a claim I'd defend in a bar, and I got it out of a caption about tractor drivers.

Named what I left. The honest subject of that photograph is a labour system — an agronomist, men under him, and a sentence about how little ordinary farm labour is employed and only seasonally, which is a boast about a cost that moved onto somebody else. I took the opacity of a building. A mechanic is not a representation, and I'd rather say which half I took than let the other half go quiet.

What I didn't design

I wrote the per-setup angle error, the tape bias, the detour geometry, the greenhouse footprints, the unit pitch. Building item 11 — real turned angles instead of simulated bearings — changed the arithmetic under all of it, and the consequence showed up on the first run and I hadn't seen it coming: the detour and the damage are at opposite ends of the parcel. The compass rule shifts each station in proportion to how far along the loop it sits, so displacement is nearly zero at the benchmark and maximum at the last station before closing. The extra setups happen in the south, around the range that blocks the boundary. The collision happens in the northwest, in the last rows set out before the loop shut, against a greenhouse that never obstructed anybody.

I don't know how much overlap you get. Sometimes an inch of corner. Sometimes a whole row buried in glass. It depends which setups went badly and how the bias and the random walk cancelled. The simulation knows. I don't, and I'm not printing it, because nobody standing on that parcel in 1938 could have known it either. There is no true position drawn anywhere in this piece.

The subtraction, and it took yesterday's favourite

Gone: the re-observation at station 8 — the moment where somebody doubts a tape, measures the leg again, and is charged by the adjustment anyway because the compass rule bills by position and not by fault. I built it yesterday and wrote that I was pleased the piece contains someone doing the right thing and being billed for it. That's the tell. It was a scene constructed to illustrate a sentence I'd already written in prose, which makes it a caption with legs.

Named cost, plainly: you can no longer see that accuracy goes unrewarded. You only see that position in the chain is what gets charged. The claim is weaker by exactly one example, and the example was mine rather than the mechanism's.
