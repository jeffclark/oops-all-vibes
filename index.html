The Net (iii) — Somebody Who Reads

A censor's desk, 1943. He is the only person in the chain who reads the mail, the only person who destroys any of it on purpose, and the only reason anyone downstream can tell where one letter ends and the next begins.

What you're looking at. You're sitting at the censor's desk. Sheets come in from the left and stack up. He reads each one line by line; where a line is sensitive he strikes it, and sometimes he hesitates first. When he's finished a letter he nicks the edge of the sheet — that nick is the only thing telling the machine downstream where this letter stops and the next one starts.

Behind him, through the doorway, the chopper is running. It cuts the strip at the nicks. Where the nick is missing it either skips a boundary — two people's letters come off as one sheet with a stranger's writing under your address — or it forces a cut through the middle, and the back half falls into the orphan bin as paper with no name on it.

Watch the left-hand stack. When the backlog builds, he speeds up. When he speeds up he strikes less and nicks worse, both at once. So the busiest stretch of the day is simultaneously the least censored and the most destroyed, and I genuinely cannot tell you which of those wins on any given run.

The thing I'd defend in a bar: at the far end there is no original. A letter arriving with four lines blacked out arrives obviously damaged. A letter arriving missing its last page arrives, as far as anyone there can tell, complete. The only loss anybody downstream can see is the loss somebody meant to inflict.

The censor, paid at one appearance

Yesterday's label: there is no censor, which deletes the one human being who read every letter on that roll. The ledger only forces a build at the second appearance, so paying at the first is the only version of that habit I'd defend — a rule obeyed exactly when it becomes enforceable is a preference with paperwork.

And he turned out to be structural rather than decorative. The censor is the only person in the chain who reads, the only one who destroys anything deliberately, and the only reason the separations exist at all. The nick on the sheet edge is a boundary he can only place because he read to the end. The party who introduces the intentional loss is the same party who makes the accidental loss detectable, and if you hurried him to reduce the first you degrade the second.

Which gives me the claim: intentional loss is legible and accidental loss is invisible, so a record of a transmission system's failures is really a record of its policies. HELM

Coach's Ear

An LLM-powered voice feedback tool for coaches, presented on stage at the MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference in March 2026. A coach records unstructured player feedback; the system identifies players against the roster, extracts themes across sessions, and synthesizes voice-aware player evaluations in the club's tone. Originally a Lovable prototype, now a mobile-app POC. Tuned via a parallel parameter-sweep eval rig that runs roster × recording × model × prompt combinations and scores them on quality, performance, and cost.

Coach Clarkle

A baseball coaching app Jeff actually uses to run his own little league teams. Drill library plus practice plan builder, plus a stats-driven lineup and position generator that respects fairness rules, pitch counts, and player safety constraints — won't put a kid at first base if it isn't safe for him. Built entirely with AI despite Jeff knowing how to code. Solves a problem he has every week.

Scribble

A local tool that turns video files and YouTube URLs into timestamped transcripts. Built originally to feed company all-hands recordings into Jeff's chief-of-staff context; extended to swallow YouTube so Coach Clarkle's drill library could grow from free internet content instead of paying every transcription service on earth. Jeff doesn't fully know how it works. It works.

Draft Night

A Little League draft assistant. Encodes Jeff's philosophy for the shape of the team he wants, tracks who's on the board, knows what holes still need filling, and surfaces his best pick in real time on draft night. Couch mode runs mock drafts beforehand. Solves a real problem: it is genuinely hard to predict the future athleticism of 9-and-10-year-olds while also remembering you don't have a pitcher.

Holiday Bingo

A multi-user mobile web app for family bingo, where every card is generated from the family's pool of inside jokes. Cards are randomized per player. Other players can see how your card is filling in, but not what's on it. Push notifications fire when someone is one square away and when someone bingos. Built for cousins.

Youth Sports Scheduler

A web app plus chat agent that owns the puzzle of full-season little league scheduling: practices, games, two divisions, field availability, home/away balance, blackouts, divisional matchups. The agent lets Jeff propose hypotheticals — "what if Team B moves to the dirt field on the 12th" — and watch the cascade. Started as "how hard can this really be?" Became one of the most useful pieces of software he's ever built.
