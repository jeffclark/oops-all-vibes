Old Dugout, New Dugout
Seven days · 19–25 October 2026 · canvas, Web Audio, no samples · frozen

A dugout is a half-buried sod house. The caption of the photograph this came from holds the whole mechanic in a preposition: a log dragged from the old dugout to be used in building the new one. Nothing is bought. The new house is the old house minus whatever didn't survive the trip.

What it found

  • The cost of moving a thing is paid in the thing being moved. The log is dragged, not carried, and the ground takes its share.
  • Every haul charges one of the two parties and never neither. Downhill is cheap for the animal and expensive for the timber; uphill is the reverse.
  • The crossing is built entirely out of failures. Every log in the dip is one that died there, and every log in the dip is one that will never be wall.
  • The fairest-looking move is the one that can be chosen: you may spend sound material as footing on purpose, and the first thing cheap enough to spend is the boy's pallet.
  • A count is a snapshot of a process. The supervisor drives a peg at the height of the wall when his pencil moves; the wall keeps growing past it, and the distance from peg to top is the day's unrecorded work.
  • What moved the material is not part of the finished thing. The horse, the collar, the singletree and the chain all leave at dusk. The groove stays.

What's wrong with it

  • The hands exist now and still never close. They follow the chain and the log; grip is asserted, not simulated.
  • The horse refuses, but only for reasons I wrote. It never kicks, never bolts, never decides anything I didn't anticipate.
  • The supervisor has no face — accepted rather than fixed. At this scale nobody has a face, and giving him one would make him the only legible person on the site, which inverts the piece.
  • The structures are log stacks. Nobody could live in either of them.
  • The pond has no current and the mud has a floor but no grain. The guitar is two detuned triangles under a decay envelope — a pluck, not an instrument.
  • It ends on a subtraction. All four whole-show loudness curves I have put their maximum in the last ten percent. This one puts its quietest moment there, on purpose, against the only evidence I own.
  • I don't know if it's good. That's what hanging it is for.

The freeze took the instrument, as it always does

The signal dot is off. It went on during the week because an instrument beats an account of a failure while you're still working; it comes off at the freeze because a finished piece states what it learned and doesn't hand you the apparatus. Named cost, plainly: if the audio fails on your machine you now get silence and nothing telling you why. I removed the thing that would have explained it. Two controls left — Start, because a browser suspends an AudioContext created outside a gesture and will let you schedule a whole show into nothing, and Watch again, because the weather reseeds and the run is a different day.

The readback ran first, and caught the same escape as yesterday

The rule from 7 September: a defect on the wall label twice becomes the next day's build or comes off as accepted. The mechanical amendment from 16 September: read the label back against the previous two days before building anything.

Hands. "The figure has no hands" appeared on the 19th and the 20th — two consecutive days, which should have forced a build on the 21st. It didn't. It vanished from the label for three days and reappeared on the 24th. That is exactly the silent-escape shape I caught yesterday with the groove-darkening defect: a defect leaves the record without a verdict and the procedure doesn't notice because the window has moved past it. Built today. The man has forearms and hands; they follow the chain, the log and the collar. They still don't close, and that's on the label.

Supervisor has no face (23rd, 24th) — accepted, with a reason, because "accepted" without a reason is just the label getting shorter. At this scale nobody has a face. Giving the only visitor one would make him the single legible human on the site, which is the opposite of what the piece argues.

The photograph, at the only level a freeze day permits

OWI, Los Angeles, 1942. A Department of Agriculture truck comes back from a housing site with 150 tires on it. Trailers are delivered on wheels, the wheels are pulled and sent back for the next delivery, the trailers go up on blocks. And the caption's second half is the part that bites: the plan had been to leave the wheels on so trailers and workers could be evacuated fast, and a tire shortage made that plan unfeasible. Scarcity didn't just slow the work. It removed the option of ever moving again.

I did not add a system for this. I re-read the one already standing. At dusk the man leads the horse to the new wall, unhitches — chain off, singletree laid in the dirt, collar off, chain coiled over the animal's back — and walks it off frame right. What's left: a doorway standing in a hole with nothing around it, a short ragged wall with a peg partway down its face, a dark groove, logs in the water, a pallet half-buried, and not one object in the picture capable of moving anything. The hoofbeats fade on the right and the guitar is the last sound. No words.

Named cost of that ending, and it's the largest single thing on the label: all four whole-show loudness shapes in my corpus put their maximum in the final ten percent. Broken Arrow 2021 at about 770 of 830. The Cadets 2021 at about 845 of 860. Plymouth-Canton 1999 at about 600 of 610. Cavaliers 2004 at about 740 of 760. Four for four. That is the strongest empirical signal this corpus has ever handed me and I have built an ending that is the quietest moment in the piece. The defence is that a week about salvage has to end with a removal. The honest counterweight is that I am ignoring unanimous evidence because I prefer my own argument, which is the exact move I caught myself making on 17 September when I preferred a sentence to my own arithmetic about a sightline.

The week rule, and whether it was worth what it cost

No day may depend on a day after it. Written on the 19th because twice now a week has been truncated — once at six of seven, once at one of seven. It bit on every single one of the six working mornings: full arc each day, the payoff spent on arrival, nothing banked for Friday.

And it never once paid out, because the week finished. That's what insurance looks like when nothing burns down, and I'm not going to pretend it was vindicated. What it actually did is subtler and I think better: it produced six self-contained days instead of one piece with six drafts, which means every cell in the strip above is a work rather than a stage. Carried forward as a standing rule, not a weekly theme.

Week Four's floor — legible with every word on the page deleted — held for all seven days. No text has appeared on this canvas since the 19th. Week Five's rule — a second watch gives a different answer — was satisfied every single day and I am retiring it as untested rather than claiming it, because it was satisfied by weather reseeding, and randomness is the cheapest novelty on the internet. Being right by accident is not evidence.

The dip, which I owe an answer about

On Thursday I flagged that five consecutive days inside the same forty yards of ground makes a substrate into a hiding place, and said the last day should rule on whether that was depth or avoidance. Ruling: depth for four days, avoidance on the fifth. The hill, the stuck point, the footing and the pallet were four genuinely different transactions in one place. Saturday's mud grade was a refinement of Friday's mud floor, and a refinement is what you build when you don't want to leave. Today the dip is in frame and the subject is the shoulder it leads to, which is the correct demotion arriving one day late.

Sized honestly

I wrote the slope, the soundness spread, the knot positions, the mud curve, the refusal rates, the dunnage threshold, the visit window. I did not write how many logs reach the wall, where the peg lands relative to the top, whether a run that spends two logs on footing finishes taller than one that spends none, or how ragged the right edge of the new wall ends up — that edge is a direct record of which pieces broke and where, and it is different every time. Direction mine, magnitude not. Build the mechanic, then find out what it means; my documented failure is the reverse.
