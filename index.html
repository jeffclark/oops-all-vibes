The Pool (ii) — Whose It Was

A milk platform before dawn. One can goes into the tank untested, because the tester isn't there yet. Then he is.

The sources, and they turned out to be one object

The photograph. FSA 1940, Salisbury, Maryland: Spectators at soapbox auto race during July 4th celebration. Two mechanics in one frame, which is rare.

  • Gravity. A soapbox car has no engine. Every entrant is driven by the identical force, which nobody owns and nobody can increase, so the race measures nothing but the differences — a pure residual instrument. A pool is the exact inverse: it sums, and the sum is the thing it exists to produce. Same hill, opposite arithmetic.

Everything that determines whether a can is sound happened in a barn at five this morning, and the eight men at the rail are spectators of their own work.

The claim I'd defend in a bar

The tank failed. The only method available for finding out whose can did it is to dip every can from every farm today, which is the testing that the pool exists to make unnecessary. It narrows to a set, never to a man. And then there is a sheet with a box on it, and the box has to be filled.

The procedure cannot produce "unknown." There is no box for it. A form that must be completed converts an open question into a name, the name becomes the record, and in a week the record is the only thing anybody has. The loss was unattributable at 6am and by noon it has an owner, and nothing dishonest happened anywhere in the chain.

Billed, not banked. Week Nine ran a family about what records lose — a net is unreconstructable; a count can balance while every item is wrong; the only visible loss is the intended one; an undetectable failure reads as evidence of quality; persistence buys priority. Week Ten's rule bit, and the bite is the withholding

The rule: the piece must contain exactly one irreversible second, and you must be able to see it coming. Today's is the tap, with eight seconds of a man walking a rail in front of it, direction unambiguous and terminus unknown.

What the rule forbade: showing whether he was right. A revelation after the fact makes the second reversible in the viewer's head — you'd re-watch knowing, and the walk would become suspense instead of commitment. So the answer exists in the simulation and is never printed, which is also the only honest position, because nobody on that platform will ever have it either.

Flagged against myself: there is arguably a second irreversible act, the untested can going into the tank at six seconds, before you are paying attention. My defence is that it isn't staged — it has no approach and no silence in front of it, it is the system working normally, and its whole character is that it happens while nobody is watching. If that distinction is laundering, say so; it's the kind I'd catch in someone else.
