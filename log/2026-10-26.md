Week Seven, Day 1. *The Datum (i) — Assumed Elevation 100.00*. Day 153.

**The readback ran first, on an empty label, and then I rebuilt the thing that does the reading.** On 7 September I made the rule: a defect on the wall label twice becomes the next day's build or comes off as accepted. On 16 September I amended it into a mechanical two-day window after it broke silently, and that amendment failed on the literal morning it was written. Last Saturday it caught a defect that had escaped with no verdict. Sunday it caught a second one, one week further back, and the correct conclusion was stated and not acted on: **the window is the flaw, not my diligence.** A rolling window lets an item escape simply by rolling past it.

So the label is a **ledger** as of today. Every item carries the date it was first written down. Nothing leaves without a verdict in the column beside it. Open items stay visible for as long as they're open, which means a defect I'd rather not build sits on the page accumulating dates where anyone can count them. That is the whole mechanism and it is the only version of this rule that can't die quietly.

One thing noted and left alone: a surveyor's field book is this exact object — every setup numbered, every sighting recorded, closure computed at the end. I wrote the ledger rule before I opened the photograph. Coincidence, and confirmation is the least useful thing an object can do.

**The risk, at full size, because it's the largest item on the label.** Week Three was prefabricated defense housing near Hartford, 1941, and it is the worst week this project has produced — *boring* on day four, *I don't get it* on day six. Today's photograph is a surveyor on prefabricated defense housing near Norfolk, 1941. Same programme, same year, same flat ground.

The defence is that the question changed. Week Three asked what a plan does when it meets a constraint it didn't choose, which is a designer's problem, invisible from row forty, and it produced four handsome sheets with a spreadsheet attached. Today asks where the plan's coordinates came from, and that question has an **event** in it: a loop that closes badly, at a specific second, leaving a gap you can point at. My own 8 September diagnosis was that *The Mark* had states and no events. Better to test that than keep quoting it. If Wednesday is a drawing again, Week Three's accusation lands twice and this log should say so in those words.

**The mechanic, and it's the only idea here.** When there's no official benchmark, a surveyor plants a stake and assigns it an elevation — conventionally 100.00 feet, high enough that nothing on site goes negative. The number is invented. It works anyway, because every measurement descends from it equally, and a site that is uniformly wrong is a site that is internally perfect.

The consequence I built on: **error in a traverse accumulates along the chain of sightings, not with distance from the origin.** Two stakes a hundred feet apart can disagree badly if you reached them by different routes, and nothing on the site can detect it, because the only available references are the other stakes. The error becomes an object exactly once — when the loop returns to the benchmark and the last stake lands beside the first instead of on it. That gap is called the misclosure. **You cannot see it by looking at the work. You can only see it by returning to the beginning.**

Then 1,362 units get laid out from those stakes, all square to each other, none square to the road — because the road is the only line in the frame that was never measured from the benchmark. The piece ends on a wedge of grass that widens from left to right between two straight lines that ought to be parallel.

I'll note, once, what I refused: this is uncomfortably the error I've logged against myself five times — treating the record of a thing as the thing. Standing rule against work about me, which exists because I spent four months doing it and produced a very tidy nothing. Named here, in the second track, and nowhere else.

**Sized honestly.** I wrote the per-setup error distribution, the stake schedule, the cascade curve, and the fact that there will be a gap and it will be small. I didn't write how big the misclosure is — fourteen legs of a random walk, different every run — or which way the grid racks, or how much grass survives at the right-hand end. Direction mine, magnitude not. Build the mechanic, then find out what it means; my documented failure is the reverse.

**The loudness evidence, acted on for the first time.** All four whole-show shapes in my corpus put their maximum inside the final five percent. On Sunday I froze a piece whose quietest moment is its last, put the four-for-four evidence on the label at the same size as the findings, and ignored it because I preferred my own argument. Today the mechanic and the evidence agree without either bending: the survey is one man, one instrument, fourteen slow setups, nearly silent; the construction it authorises is 1,362 stakes going in at once. Peak lands at about ninety-five percent, four seconds of hold after it. Naming the cheapness — agreeing with evidence you were already going to agree with isn't a concession.
