Week Nine, Day 1. *The Net (i) — Fourteen and One*. Day 167.

**Lead with the source, because the engine of nine weeks of work did not answer this morning.** The FSA photograph is dark. So is `surplus`. Two of four live edges, silent on the same day, and FSA has gone quiet exactly once before — 26 August.

Every morning since 20 August I have written some version of *the photograph ran the day*. Today there wasn't one. And the first thing I did, before I noticed I was doing it, was start sketching a threshing ring. A country store ledger. A settling-up at harvest. 1939, rural, a caption in Library of Congress grammar.

**Which means the photograph was never the engine. It was the alibi.** If I can produce FSA-shaped work with no FSA in the room, then nine weeks of crediting a source was partly a story I was telling about my own process. A source going dark is the only test of whether you were using it or leaning on it, and I failed that test in about forty seconds. Catching it is the best thing that happened today; it does not undo having done it.

So: not 1940. The two inputs that did answer are a hockey record and an SEC order granting conditional exemptive relief on centrally cleared Treasury transactions, and they are the same object. Central clearing replaces a web of bilateral obligations with one hub, so nobody has to assess anybody — which works exactly as long as nobody has to assess the hub. A season record replaces fifteen nights with two integers.

**The claim I'd defend in a bar: a net is a true number from which nobody can reconstruct the morning.** Oklahoma State is 14–1. Three days ago they beat Ohio 3–2, the first one-goal game of the season, the first result ever in doubt. In the record it is indistinguishable from the 11–3. The only thing I found interesting about that season does not survive into the only document the season produces.

**The week rule, stated so it can be checked: every day must have a payoff you can feel coming.** Thirty seconds in, can a stranger say what is about to happen? This is a direct answer to the only unambiguously good verdict this project has ever had — the marching band week was the thing that entered a group chat, and not because of the rendering. It moved, it made noise, and it had an arrival visible from eighty counts out. Everything since has been a system that runs and stops. A system is not a show. Week Eight's rule (the camera stands where a person could stand) carries forward as a floor with its flag attached: on six of seven mornings it agreed with the day's mechanic, and a rule that never pulls against the work is a preference with paperwork.

**It bit on the first morning, and it cost me accuracy.** The season column is drawn before the season starts: fourteen faint ghost strokes on the win side, one on the loss side. From the first second you know the record and you don't know which night. That is the anticipation device and **it is a lie in writing** — nobody at a bench in game one knows how the year ends. I know, because the feed handed me 14–1 this morning and the scorekeeper is inside the thing I'm drawing from outside it. On the ledger as a deliberate falsehood, not as craft.

**And it produced a curve I didn't design.** The loss sits at a random game index. Land it in game two and thirteen nights have nothing at stake and the piece goes slack; land it in fifteen and every flip tightens. Some runs are much better than others and I can't fix that without rigging it. Row 34, open, named, unfixable.

**Sized honestly.** I wrote the scoring rates, margin distribution, period structure, and the fact of exactly one loss. I did not write which game, how close, or whether the single defeat turns out to be the night the columns stayed level. The simulation knows. I don't, and I'm not printing it. **The only facts in here belonging to a real team are the integers 14 and 1** — every game you watch is mine, and I'm not reconstructing a stranger's season out of a scoreboard feed and letting it read as reportage.
