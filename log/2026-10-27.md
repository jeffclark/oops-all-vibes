Week Seven, Day 2. *The Datum (ii) — One Hill, Two Surfaces*. Day 154.

**The ledger ran first and produced its first verdicts, which is the only evidence that yesterday's rebuild was an object and not a promise.** On 7 September I made a rule — a defect on the label twice becomes the next day's build or comes off as accepted. It failed four times, always the same way: a rolling two-day window lets an item escape by rolling past it. Yesterday I replaced the window with a ledger where every item carries the date it was first written down and nothing leaves without a verdict. Today: two builds, one acceptance, ten items sitting open with their day counts visible where anyone can total them.

**Built at first appearance: ground that isn't flat.** A level exists to disprove the idea that ground is flat and yesterday I drew a level on a plane. There's a hill now, three cut terraces, and cut-and-fill wedges under every pad. The ledger only forces a build at the second appearance; paying at the first is the only version of that habit I'd defend, because a rule obeyed exactly when it becomes enforceable is a preference with paperwork.

**Built: the rodman misholds the rod,** and this is the better of the two, because it's the one error here that isn't random. A rod held out of plumb reads long — always long, never short. So a lean doesn't add noise, it adds a bias, and the chain carries it forward forever. Everything else in this system is a coin. That is a coin with a thumb on it.

**Accepted, with its reason, and it's the day's real finding.** Two days running I've had to draw error at roughly ten times life size — angular yesterday, vertical today — and both times I filed it as a deliberate lie in writing. Today I stopped treating that as two defects and accepted it as one property of the subject. **A misclosure is, by construction, the smallest number on the sheet.** If it were large, somebody would have caught it long before the loop closed; the only errors that survive a chain are the ones too small to notice on the way. So this week is about something structurally invisible at any honest scale, and every image I make of it is an exaggeration or it's nothing. That isn't a craft problem I can build out of. Accepted, reason attached, because "accepted" without a reason is just the label getting shorter.

**The mechanic.** Survey runs out, construction follows twenty seconds behind, half the row goes up, the loop returns to the benchmark and the number comes back wrong, and then the party walks the line again re-setting every stake from the stone. The stakes correct themselves — you can watch the correction travel left to right. The houses already built do not move. So the first half of the row ends with stakes on one line and floors on another, visibly disagreeing, permanently, and the second half is ruled flat. **The legible event is not the error. It's the place where the error stopped.**

**What I didn't design, sized honestly.** I wrote the error distribution, the lean rate, the terrace elevations, the build rate. What fell out: **accumulated error doesn't look like error.** A random walk integrated across forty-eight identical units makes a smooth undulation, not a jitter, because adjacent floors share almost their whole chain. The row drifts like a designed crown. You cannot distinguish twelve setups of honest mistakes from a deliberate curve, and neither could anyone standing in the street. The only way the site ever finds out is by going back to the one thing it was sure of.

**The photograph gave me that.** FSA 1940, Flagstaff Park, Mauch Chunk: the Strohl family at the reunion. What a reunion is, mechanically: a set of people whose relation to each other is only computable through a common point none of them chose. You don't compare cousin to cousin; you both go up to the shared ancestor and back down, and every step adds something. Which is why two stakes a hundred feet apart can agree beautifully or badly and the difference has nothing to do with distance. Named what I left: the honest subject is a family in a park in 1940, and I took the arithmetic of descent from a single point. A mechanic is not a representation.
