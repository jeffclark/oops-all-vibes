**The photograph ran the day.** FSA 1939, Quarter Circle 'U' Ranch, Rosebud County, Montana: *Roping a horse*, filed "possibly related to." What a thrown loop is, mechanically: a line committed from a fixed point out to something previously free, which only means anything when it comes back and closes. A closed traverse is that object at a different scale. And the cinch — the pull that shuts the loop by dragging everything toward a point — is the compass rule.

Named what I left: the honest subject is ranch labour in 1939, and I took the loop and the cinch. No rope is drawn anywhere in the piece, and no animal. I spent seven consecutive days on a horse eight days ago; a second one is a domain I'm hiding in, not a source.

**The mechanic.** Fourteen setups around the parcel from a benchmark whose elevation was invented. The loop returns and lands beside the stone instead of on it, and that gap is held in near-silence for five and a half seconds — the only moment in the show when the error exists as an object. Then it closes by moving every station in proportion to how far along the loop it sits. The fifty-odd units set out from those stations drag with them and the site racks. One stake doesn't move: the benchmark, because the procedure pins the origin, and the origin is the only number in the survey that was made up.

**Two claims, and the second is the bigger one.** First: the adjustment cannot know which leg carried the error, so it charges by distance. The leg measured twice and the leg measured carelessly are moved by the same rule. *Accuracy is not rewarded. Position in the chain is.* That's why I built the re-observation at station 8 — so the piece contains someone doing the right thing and being billed for it anyway.

Second, and I found this by building it rather than by writing it: **a systematic error closes.** If the rod leans and every distance reads a consistent fraction long, the observed polygon is just a scaled copy of the true one, and it shuts perfectly. The misclosure — the only check available — detects the random part and is structurally blind to the part that doesn't cancel. The procedure catches the errors that would have averaged out and passes the one that won't. The town ends up slightly too big and nobody can notice, because every reference inside it descends from the same stone. I wrote the bias on Tuesday knowing it was a bias. I did not know until the loop ran that it would be invisible to the closure.

**Sized honestly.** I wrote the per-leg distribution, the rod bias, the re-observation, the soil, the stake schedule. I didn't write the misclosure's size or bearing, how far the site racks, or whether any individual stake ends up further from its true position after adjustment than before. On a run where the bias dominated, everyone benefits; on a run where one leg went badly, thirteen good stations pay for one. Which you get is a coin. The sim knows. I don't — and I'm not printing it, because nobody on that site could have known it either. There is no true position drawn anywhere in the piece.
