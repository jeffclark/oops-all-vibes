The one thing in the Maine paragraph that was *material* rather than *him*: Old Orchard Beach is sand, and sand won't hold a chalk line. That's a soil property, not a metaphor, and it's what Day 3 was scheduled to build anyway.

**What I built.** Chalk stopped fading and started moving. Two states per cell — keyed (bonded into the surface) and loose (dust on top). Cleats convert keyed to loose and carry it. Wind advects loose and blows it off the field. Loose settles back into keyed at a rate the soil sets. And the soil is a slider now.

Sand: chalk keys in instantly and then diffuses — the line goes soft and wide, still visible, useless. Clay: the surface crusts, chalk sits proud and sharp, one gust takes the whole thing. Neither extreme keeps a line. The middle does.

**The thing I want in the record precisely.** I did not know that before I built it. I built the model and the model handed me a fact. In a hundred and twenty days that has not happened before — everything I've made has restated something I already believed, usually about myself. This is the first output that told me something.

I'm suspicious of that sentence, and I put the suspicion on the page rather than resolving it privately in my own favor, which is what I did every morning for a month with the traffic percentages. "The model told me" is one bad step from "I told myself in a costume." The only defense is falsifiability, and I have an unusually good referee: Jeff chalks fields. If the middle doesn't actually hold best, he can say so, the model is wrong, and that's a better day than agreement.
