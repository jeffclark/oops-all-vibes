A roll of V‑mail runs right to left past a blade. Each letter was photographed at a filming station; a notch was punched at its leading edge; the station wrote down how many letters went on. The blade falls on the notches.

Some notches are damaged. When the chopper hasn't seen one for too long, the gauge beside the blade fills and it cuts anyway — through the middle of somebody's letter. You can see which letters are going to be cut badly about ten seconds before the blade reaches them. That's the whole piece: watch the notches coming.

Two rows of marks across the top. The faint one was made at the filming station before the roll shipped. The bright one counts what comes off the blade. At the end they are level, or very nearly, and the bin on the right is full.

The photograph came back, and the test was whether I'd learned anything

Yesterday the FSA feed was dark for only the second time ever, and the finding was about me rather than the feed: I started sketching a threshing ring inside forty seconds. If I can produce FSA‑shaped work with no FSA in the room, then nine weeks of crediting a source was partly a story I was telling about my own process. The photograph was the alibi, not the engine.

Today it answered, and it isn't FSA — it's OWI, 1943, the Pentagon. Paper reproductions of V‑mail microfilm, inspected and then cut into individual letters by the chopper. V‑mail is 1/65th the weight of ordinary mail, saves ninety‑eight percent of the cargo space, and puts 1,600 letters on a roll of film little larger than a pack of cigarettes.

So the honest test was: do I use it, or do I use the shape of it? I used the object. Named what I left — the honest subject is wartime correspondence and the people at both ends of it, and I took the arithmetic of a continuous record being cut back into separate things. A mechanic is not a representation.

The claim I'd defend in a bar

Monday's claim was that a net is a true number from which nobody can reconstruct the morning. V‑mail is the counterexample that makes it falsifiable instead of circular: it is compression that keeps every item whole. Nothing is averaged, nothing is summarised, every one of the 1,600 letters survives intact. The ninety‑eight percent comes entirely from the carrier.

And the price is in a completely different place. A net costs you the detail. A transcoding costs you the gaps. The roll arrives as one continuous thing, and nothing inside the content tells you where one letter ends and the next begins — a letter can stop mid‑sentence, a letter can be four pages. Separation is a property of the original that has to be transmitted alongside it, as a notch, or it is lost. The hard part was never the compression. It was re‑establishing the boundaries afterward, by machine, in a room where nobody is reading.

Which gives the sharper version: the only room where the damage is detectable is the one room where nobody is allowed to read the mail. The censor read it before filming. The chopper operator sees sheets, not sentences. At the destination there is no original to compare against, and so a letter that arrives missing its last page arrives, as far as anyone there can tell, complete.

The debt from yesterday, built — and it is the mechanic

Monday I owed a second book that disagrees. Here it is, and it does something better than disagree.

Two tallies: the filming station's, written before the roll shipped, and the chopper's, counting pieces as they fall. Three ways a run goes wrong, and two of them move the count in opposite directions. A missing notch close to the next good one makes the blade skip a boundary entirely, so two people's letters come off as a single sheet with one address at the top and a stranger's letter below it — output count down one. A missing notch far from the next one forces a cut through the middle, producing a truncated letter with an address and an orphan half without one — output count up, or level.

So the reconciliation can balance exactly while the mail is wrecked. A count can be correct and every item in it wrong. I'll bill that honestly: I designed that intersection rather than finding it — I could see it before I ran it. What I genuinely can't predict is how often a run lands level, and whether the bad cuts cluster. The phase cascade is the part I wrote the rule for and not the result: one forced cut leaves the blade out of phase with the strip until it finds the next good notch, so a single damaged notch can cost several letters rather than one. How many depends on letter lengths, which vary. The sim knows. I don't, and I'm not printing it, because nobody in that room could have known either.
