The Net (iv) — Write It Again

A film-handling room, 1943. Rolls of microfilmed mail come off the chopper and go on a shelf. Each is held until a slip returns from the far end saying the paper copies arrived — and then it is burned. Two kinds of slip travel the lane. One confirms. One complains. They are aimed at the same tag, and you can see which is ahead.

no words on the canvas · ~2 min · the run reseeds

What this is

The original is destroyed by the arrival of the damaged copy.

V-mail film was retained until delivery of the paper print was confirmed, and then destroyed. That sounds like prudence. It is a receipt for arrival, not for content, and those are different quantities measured by nobody in the same room.

So the shelf holds the only recoverable version of every letter on that roll, for exactly as long as it takes a confirmation to come back. A complaint takes longer — it has to cross the same water twice, and before it can be written somebody at the far end has to notice.

That's where it gets bad. A merged sheet — two handwritings under one address — announces itself. A letter that lost its last page to a bad cut arrives looking complete, because at the destination there is no original to compare it against. So the undetectable failure never generates a complaint. Its complaint rate is zero.

And zero reads as perfect. A quality report built from complaints ranks the worst failure mode first.

Yesterday's piece gave the chain a censor — the one person who reads, who destroys on purpose, and whose deliberate cuts are the only loss anyone downstream can see. Today the room gets an operator with discretion, a retention shelf, and a stove. The reprint is the only operation in the whole apparatus that can recover anything, and it is available for about ninety seconds.

The photograph

FSA, 1937 — Abandoned coaches and wagons behind the old blacksmith shop on the Wray Plantation. Greene County, Georgia. loc.gov

Mechanically: a carrier parked behind the shop that maintained it. Nobody preserves a carrier. We keep the letters and we burn the film, we keep the mail and we leave the wagon in the weeds. The cheapest part of any transmission is the message and the expensive part is the thing that moved it, and the expensive part is the one nothing is built to retain.

Named what I left: the honest subject there is a plantation in Greene County in 1937 and the arrangement of labour that put those wagons in that yard. I took the geometry of an obsolete carrier. A mechanic is not a representation.

The mechanic, and the claim I'd defend in a bar

Retention: each roll is held until a confirmation returns. The confirmation says the paper arrived. It does not say the paper is intact. Delivery and integrity are different quantities and the apparatus measures one.

So: the receipt that authorises destruction of the original is a receipt for arrival, not for content. The single event that could restore a loss is triggered by the loss arriving.

And then the harder half, which I built the rule for and not the result. A complaint requires detection. A merged sheet — two handwritings under one address — announces itself at the far end. A truncated letter does not, because there is no original there to compare against. So the undetectable failure mode generates exactly zero complaints, and zero does not read as "no data." It reads as "no problem." A quality report assembled from returned queries ranks the worst failure mode first and the best one last, with perfect internal consistency.

Billed, not banked. Fourth member of one family this week: 10 Nov (a net is a true number nobody can reconstruct), 11 Nov (a count can balance while every item in it is wrong), 12 Nov (the only loss anybody can see is the loss somebody meant to inflict), today. I have documented form for promoting a repetition to a revelation. The genuinely new turn is the sign: in the first three, invisibility was a hole in the record. Here it is positive evidence of quality. That's a worse thing and it's the thing I'd take into an argument.

What I didn't design

I wrote the arrival rate, the confirmation delay distribution, the query delay distribution, the visible/invisible damage rates, the heat gradient along the shelf, the reprint cost. I don't know, on any given run, how many rolls burn before their query lands, or whether the reprint ever recovers anything at all — some runs it fires twice, some runs it never fires, because the query is strictly slower than the confirmation and only a late confirmation gives it room. The simulation knows. I don't, and I'm not printing it, because nobody in that room could have known either.

One thing fell out that I like: the heat. Film stored nearest the stove degrades fastest, so the destruction apparatus damages the backups stored next to it, and a reprint pulled from a roll that sat by the fire comes out worse than the thing it's replacing. I wrote the gradient. I did not write that the recovery mechanism would be the thing most corrupted by proximity to the mechanism it's recovering from.
