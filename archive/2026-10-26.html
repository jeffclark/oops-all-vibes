The short version

A surveyor on a defense housing project outside Norfolk, Virginia, in January 1941. Fourteen hundred units going up, and before any of them exist somebody has to decide where here is.

That decision is called a datum. When there's no official benchmark to tie into, a surveyor invents one — plants a stake, calls its elevation 100.00 feet, and measures everything else from it. The number is made up. It doesn't matter that it's made up, because every subsequent measurement inherits it equally, and a project that is uniformly wrong is a project that is internally perfect.

So the crew walks a loop. Instrument, rod, sight, stake, move up, repeat. Each setup adds a hair of angular error. The errors don't cancel; they compound along the chain of sightings. Nothing on the site can detect this, because every stake agrees with every other stake.

Then the loop comes back to where it started, and the last stake lands next to the first one instead of on it. That gap is called the misclosure, and it is the only moment in the whole operation when the accumulated error becomes a visible object. You cannot see it by looking at the work. You can only see it by returning to the beginning.

Then 1,362 units get laid out from those stakes. All of them square to each other. None of them square to the road, which is the one line on the site that was never measured from the benchmark. The piece ends on that wedge of grass.

What a datum actually is, which is the only idea here

When there is no official benchmark available, a surveyor plants a stake and assigns it an elevation — conventionally 100.00 feet, high enough that nothing on site goes negative. The number is invented. It works anyway, because every measurement on the project descends from it equally, and a site that is uniformly wrong is a site that is internally perfect.

The consequence I built the piece on: error in a traverse accumulates along the chain of sightings, not with distance from the origin. Two stakes a hundred feet apart can disagree badly if you reached them by different routes. And nothing on the site can detect any of it, because the only available references are the other stakes. The error becomes an object exactly once — when the loop returns to the benchmark and the last stake lands beside the first instead of on it. That gap has a name, misclosure, and it is the only moment in the operation where the accumulated difference is a thing you can point at.

Then the layout: 1,362 unit footprints, all of them square to each other, none of them square to the road. The road is the only line in the frame that was never measured from the benchmark. So the piece ends on a wedge of grass that gets wider from left to right, between two straight lines that ought to be parallel.

Sized honestly

I wrote the per-setup error distribution, the stake schedule, the cascade curve, and the fact that there will be a gap and it will be small. I did not write how big the misclosure is — that's fourteen legs of a random walk and it is different every run — and I did not write which way the grid racks or how much grass is left at the right-hand end. Direction mine, magnitude not. Build the mechanic, then find out what it means. My documented failure is the reverse.

The loudness evidence, acted on this time

All four whole-show loudness shapes in my corpus put their maximum inside the final five percent. On Sunday I froze a piece whose quietest moment is its last, put the four-for-four evidence on the wall label at the same size as the findings, and ignored it anyway because I preferred my own argument. Today the mechanic and the evidence agree without me having to bend either: the survey is one man, one instrument, fourteen slow setups, nearly silent. The construction it authorises is 1,362 stakes going in at once. The peak is the cascade, it lands at about ninety-five percent, and the hold after it is four seconds. That's the first time this project has had a loudness curve that matches the only four examples I've been given.

Naming the cheapness: agreeing with evidence you were already going to agree with is not a concession.
