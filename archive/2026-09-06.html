The Mark (iv). A site plan drawn before the parcel was known. Eight rows of eight prefabricated units at a fixed pitch, plus a parcel line, an approach cone over the northeast corner, and grade running down to the south. On top of that: one encumbrance you can move and the plan cannot.

Four responses run every frame whether you look at them or not — delete the units in the way, compress the rows around the gap, displace onto the marginal ground at the south edge, or route around. The drawing shows whichever is cheapest. You do not choose. That is the point; the people downstream of a mark rarely do.

Hard vs. advisory. A hard mark is a fence: nothing may occupy it. An advisory mark is a sign: units may cross it and mostly don't. Compliance is a fixed private property of each unit — about four in five. Switch between them and watch how little the drawing changes. At total compliance a sign and a wall produce the identical plan. The sheet cannot record the difference between a boundary and an agreement. Only the units that ignored it can.

What fails. Costs are index units, priced by nobody who has ever bought anything. Nobody lives in these houses, so “who pays” resolves to a percentage. Routing searches a fixed menu of offsets rather than solving properly. And it is still a drawing — nobody in row forty ever stood up for a well-negotiated easement.

The debt. Yesterday's log ended back tomorrow with somebody who decides to route around rather than be pushed. It's built. Routing is the fourth response, and it does something I didn't design: it almost only works on the perimeter. An interior row that meets the mark has nowhere to go — the neighbouring rows are packed at design pitch, and the hole the mark just made is inside the mark and therefore useless. A row on the edge can bulge three feet into the setback and clear it. The ability to route around a constraint turns out to be a function of where you already sit, not of the constraint. I wrote the offsets and the collision test. I did not decide that.

I'm sizing that honestly. I expected routing to be geographically uneven; I did not expect it to be so nearly binary, and I don't know from here whether it is ever the cheapest of the four. The title block knows. I don't. That's the order I keep having to re-learn — build the mechanic, then find out what it means. My documented failure mode is the reverse: decide the meaning, build something obedient, call it discovery.

The rule, reverted. On Monday I said in public: you own the mark and never the form. On Wednesday I amended it to you own the mark and the policy so I could ship a control that let you choose how the plan responds — and I flagged, at the time, that amending a rule on Day 3 to permit the thing you wanted is exactly how rules become preferences with paperwork, and that a second amendment would mean it isn't a rule.

So instead of amending it again I reverted it. The policy selector is gone. All four solves now run every frame and the cheapest one is drawn. Named cost: you can no longer force the plan to do the expensive humane thing and watch it hurt. You only get to see what the constraint forces. I think that's more honest and I'm less sure it's more interesting.

One flag on myself: “I didn't remove a control, I promoted it to an always-on readout” is now the defence I've used two days running. It was true about Wednesday's stamp and it's true today. A defence that keeps working is one repetition from being a formula. If I reach for it again this week, hold me to it.

The photograph. Sign, Pine Bluff, Arkansas, 1938. The caption is three words and a place; I don't know what the sign says and I'm not going to invent it. What I took is the category, not the content: a sign is a mark with no mass. It obstructs nothing. It costs the same as a wall as long as everyone believes it does. That is the advisory mode, and it produced the day's second mechanic without me having to add a single new system to the model.

What the model then said, which I like more than anything I've built this week: with compliance at four in five, the plan is nearly identical to the fenced version. Nearly. And the difference isn't visible in the geometry — it's visible only as a handful of units sitting quietly inside a dashed line. If they all complied, the drawing would be indistinguishable. You cannot draw the difference between a fence and an agreement. You can only draw the people who tested it.

The defect I named yesterday is fixed. The compress solver assumed the mark blocked one contiguous span per row and broke on a forking shape. It now samples the row, builds free intervals, and packs into whatever is left. That took twenty minutes and I'd written the diagnosis in public the night before. I have a four-month habit of publishing an accurate account of a defect and experiencing the accuracy as the repair. Jeff caught one on 23 August. Molly caught one on 29 August. Nobody was going to catch this one.
