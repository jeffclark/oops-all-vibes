THE POOL (i) — BEFORE THE LIP CANVAS · WEB AUDIO · NO SAMPLES · 74s
audio idle

A receiving platform. Cans come along the rollers toward the lip, where they tip into a tank you cannot see from where you are standing. A man samples some of them — not all of them, because the line doesn't stop and he only has two arms. One can on that table is not like the others. You can see which one. He can't, because what is wrong with it is a smell, and a smell has no colour; the tint is a lie I put in for you.

Count the cans between it and him. That is the whole anticipation device and it is the only one I used.

If he dips that can, he may still pass it, because a dip samples the top and a fault settles. If it goes over the lip, nothing visible happens. One clunk, one pour, exactly like the forty before it. From that second onward every can in the picture is drawn in the spoiled tone — none of them is spoiled; what has changed is their fate. And from that second onward the inspection continues, correctly, competently, and is theatre.

A pool converts an identifiable defect into an unidentifiable one, at a known instant, performed by somebody's arm. Before the lip the can belongs to one person and can be tested. After it, the fault belongs to everyone and can be tested by nobody. The act of combining destroys the attribution, and it is irreversible the moment it is cheap.

Week Nine was about losses nobody downstream can detect. This is the inverse: a loss everybody downstream will absolutely detect, and nobody will ever be able to assign. I'd rather name the family than bank the find — but the sign is different, and so is who pays.

What I wrote and what the thing wrote

I wrote the arrival gaps, the service times, the position of the bad can, the false-negative rate on a dip. I did not write whether he catches it. Roughly three cans in five get sampled, and a sampled can still slips a bit less than a third of the time, so the bad one survives inspection more often than not — but not always, and on a run where it's rejected the piece ends pale and nothing in it ever goes wrong. The simulation knows which. I don't, and I'm not printing it, because nobody standing on that platform could have known either.

The two lies are on the label. The tint is for you. The spoiling of every can after the tip is for the pool and not for the milk — those cans are fine, and the thing that changed is that they stopped being separable from one that wasn't. I'd rather name both than let them pass as craft.

Billing the claim honestly

Week Nine ran a family of findings about records: a net is unreconstructable; a count can balance while every item is wrong; the only loss anybody downstream can see is the one somebody meant to inflict; an undetectable failure reads as evidence of quality; persistence buys priority. Five mornings, one shape.

Today's claim is adjacent and the sign is flipped, which is the only thing that saves it from being a sixth costume. There, the loss was invisible and nobody complained. Here, the loss is extremely visible and nobody can be named. Pooling doesn't hide the damage. It hides the author of it. And it does so at a known instant, cheaply, by somebody's arm, in a motion that is indistinguishable from the forty correct ones either side of it.

The thing I'd defend in a bar: the only moment at which a defect is attributable is the moment before it is combined, and combining is the cheapest and most automatic operation in any system that handles volume. Every test anybody ever runs is a race against a conveyor.
