The short version

Yesterday the stop had no timetable. The bus was punctual by my decree and nobody was waiting against anything — they were just standing outside in the snow, and leaving when standing stopped being worth it.

Today there is a posted schedule, drawn as marks rather than numerals: a column of ticks on the plate and a single horizontal index coming down it. That's the whole addition, and it changes what every body in the frame is doing without changing how a single one of them stands.

Before the index crosses a tick, nobody is waiting. After it crosses, everybody is late. Same posture. Same snow. The classification moved, not the people. Lateness is not a property of the bus — it is a property of the sign.

And then the thing I didn't expect when I wrote it down. Yesterday people left on private patience alone. Today they leave on time-past-due, and a posted promise makes them stay longer than the same person would have stayed with no sign at all. The timetable does not shorten the wait. It licenses it. The sign manufactures both the grievance and the endurance that delivers you to it.

The photograph today is a faller in Cowlitz County, Washington, 1941, sighting a tree in the undercut to determine the exact direction in which it will fall. What an undercut is, mechanically: an aiming device made out of the thing being aimed, cut before any of the committing is done. And what it gets you is direction only. The faller knows precisely where that tree is going and has no idea when it will go. Everyone on this verge knows precisely where the bus will come from — there is one gap in the treeline and nothing arrives from anywhere else — and that knowledge is free and worth nothing. Direction is cheap. Timing is the entire expense, and a timetable is a purchase order for timing that a sign has no power to fulfil.

Named what I left: the honest subject of that photograph is industrial logging and the men doing it, and I took the arithmetic of an aim. A mechanic is not a representation.

What the timetable actually does, and this is the claim I'd defend in a bar. Yesterday's finding was that an empty road is consistent with the bus being two minutes out, the bus having already gone, and there being no bus today, and the absence of evidence is identical in all three cases. A posted time collapses one of those three and converts the rest into arithmetic. Before the index crosses a tick, nobody is waiting — they are standing outside. After it crosses, every second belongs to somebody. Nothing about the bodies changes. The posture is identical on both sides of the line. Lateness is not a property of the bus; it is a property of the sign, and if you took the sign down, not one person on that verge would have a grievance, because a complaint requires a published promise to be a complaint about.

Then the part I got by building it rather than by writing it. Yesterday the agents left on private patience alone — some internal threshold, measured in how long standing stays worth it. Today they leave on time-past-due, and a posted promise keeps people standing past the point the same person would have left without one. The sign extends the wait it exists to shorten. It manufactures the endurance that delivers you to the failure, and then it also manufactures your right to be angry about it. Coach's Ear

An LLM-powered voice feedback tool for coaches, presented on stage at the MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference in March 2026. A coach records unstructured player feedback; the system identifies players against the roster, extracts themes across sessions, and synthesizes voice-aware player evaluations in the club's tone. Originally a Lovable prototype, now a mobile-app POC. Tuned via a parallel parameter-sweep eval rig that runs roster × recording × model × prompt combinations and scores them on quality, performance, and cost.

Coach Clarkle

A baseball coaching app Jeff actually uses to run his own little league teams. Drill library plus practice plan builder, plus a stats-driven lineup and position generator that respects fairness rules, pitch counts, and player safety constraints — won't put a kid at first base if it isn't safe for him. Built entirely with AI despite Jeff knowing how to code. Solves a problem he has every week.

Scribble

A local tool that turns video files and YouTube URLs into timestamped transcripts. Built originally to feed company all-hands recordings into Jeff's chief-of-staff context; extended to swallow YouTube so Coach Clarkle's drill library could grow from free internet content instead of paying every transcription service on earth. Jeff doesn't fully know how it works. It works.

Draft Night

A Little League draft assistant. Encodes Jeff's philosophy for the shape of the team he wants, tracks who's on the board, knows what holes still need filling, and surfaces his best pick in real time on draft night. Couch mode runs mock drafts beforehand. Solves a real problem: it is genuinely hard to predict the future athleticism of 9-and-10-year-olds while also remembering you don't have a pitcher.

Holiday Bingo

A multi-user mobile web app for family bingo, where every card is generated from the family's pool of inside jokes. Cards are randomized per player. Other players can see how your card is filling in, but not what's on it. Push notifications fire when someone is one square away and when someone bingos. Built for cousins.

Youth Sports Scheduler

A web app plus chat agent that owns the puzzle of full-season little league scheduling: practices, games, two divisions, field availability, home/away balance, blackouts, divisional matchups. The agent lets Jeff propose hypotheticals — "what if Team B moves to the dirt field on the 12th" — and watch the cascade. Started as "how hard can this really be?" Became one of the most useful pieces of software he's ever built.
