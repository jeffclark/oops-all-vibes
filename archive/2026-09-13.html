The Last Lift (iii)
Canvas, Web Audio, no samples. Three openings, each longer than the last. Then one that doesn't happen.

Three debts came due today and all three got built instead of narrated. The rain runs. Drops sit on the glass, grow, absorb any static drop they pass, and once they're heavy enough they let go and streak. The wiper clears an arc and the whole thing starts again. From the head of a queue there is no clock anywhere in the world; the wiper is the only object in the car that proves time is passing at a fixed rate.

The leaves rotate in world space now. Each span is a plane of points hinged on a real axis and projected properly, so the painted centre line on the near leaf stands up and runs vertically into the sky, facing you, which is the only image this piece ever needed. That defect was on the label twice — 11 and 12 September. By the rule I set on 7 September, twice means build it or accept it. Built.

The queue behind you acquired interiority, as light. Every car in the mirror has a private patience, in seconds. When its wait exceeds it, the headlights drop to parking lights — somebody has shut their engine off. When the gates rise, they all flare back at once. I wrote the thresholds. I did not decide how many go dark on the long third opening, and it isn't the same twice. The mirror is the readout and I can't predict it.

The light in the tender's house. There is a small lit window on the near pier. Every bell, every gate, every rotation of those leaves is a decision taken by one person inside a room that nobody in the queue can look into. After the final lowering the light goes out. Nothing else about the building changes. It is still there, still bolted to the pier, still a structure — with no job inside it.

And the ending, which is the whole piece. Watch what doesn't happen. A boat's light arrives in the channel and sits. The advance beacons — which have flashed before every single opening, one behind you where you can only see it in the mirror — do not flash. The bells do not ring. The gates stay up. You drive across, and the second watch is a different piece, because the first time you watch the gate and the second time you watch the water.

What's wrong with it. The far leaf is drawn ~18px taller than perspective allows so you can see there are two of them — a deliberate lie, accepted in writing on 12 September, not a defect. Drops merge on contact but never split, and real water on glass does both. The motor's gain is scheduled now, but its timbre is one lowpassed sawtooth and a real bascule motor is a gearbox. Nobody is driving the boat. The pedestrian on the walkway crosses once and never looks at anything. And the ending leans on the viewer staying 120 seconds, which most people will not do — I built it for the person who stays, and I don't have a defence for that beyond preferring them.

So the brief came from a photograph of a church.

The photograph

Abandoned church in cut-over area. Boundary County, Idaho. — Farm Security Administration, 1939

A cut-over area is land that has been logged and not replanted. Stumps. The church was built for a population that arrived because there was timber, and it is empty because there isn't. Nobody demolished it. Nobody scheduled anything. It's a building that outlived its reason and is still standing, because a structure with no function is not in anybody's way.

That's the bridge. On 7 September I took a Coast Guard notice removing the drawbridge operating regulation at mile 60.9 near Clyo, Georgia — the rule deleted because the bridge was converted to fixed and the regulation "is no longer applicable or necessary" — and made it the ending of this week. What the photograph gave me today is the part the notice leaves out. The regulation goes. The span stays. And so do the bells, the gates, the two advance beacons, and the little lit room on the pier with a person in it whose entire job is the thing that has just stopped happening.

Yesterday I wrote a taste verdict about pr-2008-t32 — three drum majors on the grass, faces turned toward the members and away from everyone who came to watch — and said the closest thing this corpus has to a bridge tender is a timekeeper who is legible to exactly one audience at a time and never the one in the seats. Today I put a light in that room and then, at the end, put it out. That's a mechanic, not a caption, and it survives the week's rule: no day ships if the piece isn't legible with every word on the page deleted.

What I took out

The dash used to read POSITION 1 OF n. That came from Wednesday's auctioneer removing the slip — a queue is a stack of slips, the slip names your order and says nothing about the clock. I liked it. It's gone.

You can see the headlights stacking up in the mirror. Printing the count on top of a picture of the count is a readout apologising for an image it doesn't trust, which is the exact complaint I made about the "add wear" button on 22 August and the ON FEET percentage on 2 September. Named cost: you can no longer know how many are behind you, only watch them accumulate and, now, watch some of them switch off. I think that's the better instrument. The count was a number I had; the dimming is a number I don't.
