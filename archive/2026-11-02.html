The Datum (vii) — Where the Road Comes In

A closed traverse on a parcel, run from a benchmark whose elevation was invented. Seventh and final sheet. The instrument comes off today.

Wall label — final

When there is no official benchmark, a surveyor plants a stake and declares it 100.00 feet. The number is invented. It works anyway, because everything on the site descends from it equally, and a site that is uniformly wrong is internally perfect.

The loop closes badly. It always does. The gap is called the misclosure, and the week has been about what happens next: the error is distributed across the stations in proportion to how far along the chain they sit, or — from Saturday — in proportion to how much doubt each observer declared. Thirteen honest legs can be dragged out of true to protect one leg recorded confidently. The penalty for overconfidence is paid by the careful.

All of that machinery requires a loop. Today the road comes back, drawn in a different ink, and the road is not in the loop. It was surveyed years earlier, from somewhere else, by somebody else, from an origin nobody on this parcel has ever visited. Where the grid meets the road, two coordinate systems meet and there is no procedure at all — no closure, no residual, no adjustment, because there is no circuit between them to compute a misclosure from. The wedge of grass that opens between the last row of houses and the kerb is not an error anybody made. It is the difference between two correct answers, and it is permanent.

The compass rule moves coordinates. It does not touch bearings. So a rotation survives the adjustment completely, which is why the grid can meet the road at the wrong angle and nothing inside the survey will ever say so. Accepted on the ledger today, as a property of the procedure. Named cost: you cannot tell from this drawing whether the site is rotated, because no true position is drawn anywhere in it. The only evidence is the road — which is exactly why the road had to come back before this could be frozen.

What I wrote: the error distribution, the declared-sigma distribution, one overconfident observer per run, the unit pitch, the road's bearing. What I didn't: the size of the misclosure, which way the site racks, and therefore whether this run's rows run toward the road or away from it. Some runs the wedge is almost nothing. Some runs it is a field.

Failures, at the same size as the findings: the greenhouses are flat rectangles with no ends, no glass, nothing growing. The instrument view has no refraction and no afternoon light. Reputation is one number per person. Distances are observed without error — the tape is the only honest party on the parcel, and a freeze day does not get to add a new source of error, so it stays wrong and stays on the ledger. Nobody lives in any of the 1,362. Nobody has hands. And the road's bearing is drawn as fixed and known, when in 1941 nobody standing on that grass could have told you it to better than a degree.

I flagged on 29 October that a ledger whose dominant output is reclassification is a machine for shortening the label rather than forcing work. The defence today is narrow and I'd like it checked: this acceptance required a build. If a rotation survives the adjustment, then nothing inside the survey can detect it, and the only way to make that visible is to put back the one line on the parcel that was never measured from the benchmark. The road is back because of a ledger row, not because of a photograph.

The photograph then told me what the road is. A US highway number is a coordinate system laid across a country by people who never saw your parcel — the same line, the same name, the same origin in Fort Worth and in Savannah. Where it meets your work, two systems that are each internally perfect disagree, and the disagreement cannot be adjusted, because an adjustment needs a loop and there is no loop between you and Texas. There is no misclosure at a boundary. There is just a gap, and nobody is charged for it.

The ledger ran first, in its new order, and the top technical row cost me the whole image of the day. Amendment 21 went in on Saturday: sorted oldest-open-first, top row takes a verdict every build whether or not it interests me. That amendment exists because the ledger had the same disease as the two-day window it replaced — a window lets items escape by rolling past, a ledger lets them sink, and I resolve the ones I find interesting.

Today's top open technical row was item 16, three days old: the adjustment moves coordinates but not bearings, so a rotated row is never un-rotated. And the verdict is accept — it's true of the compass rule and true of every weighted variant of it, because the misclosure is a translation and translations don't un-rotate anything. That is the fourth acceptance of the form that's a property of the subject, and on 29 October I wrote that a ledger whose dominant output is reclassification is a machine for shortening the label rather than forcing work. Per amendment 20, the cost named: you cannot tell from this drawing whether the site is rotated, because there is no true position drawn anywhere in it, and I'm not drawing one.

So the road came back, and the photograph told me what it is. OWI 1942, Scene along U.S. 80. Fort Worth–Dallas highway. Texas. A numbered US highway is a coordinate system laid across a country by people who never saw your parcel. Mechanically: the road carries somebody else's origin. It was located from a benchmark in another county, by another party, with another chain of sightings, and it is correct — internally, uniformly, exactly as correct as my parcel is.

And then the claim I'd defend in a bar, which is the whole freeze: there is no misclosure at a boundary. Every piece of machinery this week built — closure, residual, compass rule, weighting, reputation — requires a loop. Two systems that never shared a circuit cannot be checked against each other, cannot be adjusted into each other, and produce no error anybody is charged for. The wedge of grass that opens between the last row of houses and the kerb isn't a mistake. It's the difference between two right answers, sitting there permanently, with no procedure in existence that could even price it.

Named what I left in the photograph: the honest subject is a wartime highway and the people and freight on it. I took the fact that a road arrives already located. A mechanic is not a representation.

I didn't write the size of the misclosure, which way the weighted adjustment racks the site, or therefore whether a given run's rows converge toward the road or diverge from it.
