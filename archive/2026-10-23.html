Old Dugout, New Dugout (v) — The Visit

Georgia, 23 Oct 2026. Canvas, Web Audio, ~2 min. Week Six of seven, day 5.
Possibly related to: FSA LC-USF34-8a04639, Lake of the Woods County, Minnesota, 1939.

Two structures on one slope. The left one is being eaten to feed the right one. Everything that goes up the hill is dragged there; the ground takes a share of every trip; logs that break are cut loose where they break and the ones that fall in the dip become the floor you cross on.

Somewhere in the middle of the day a second figure walks in from the right along the high ground. He never touches a log. He never goes near the dip. He counts the wall, writes, drives a peg at the height of his count, and goes. The man stops while this happens — the stoppage is the whole cost — and the supervisor does not need him to be present in order to finish.

By dusk the wall is above the peg. How far above depends on when he came. I wrote the arrival window. I did not write the gap.

What is wrong with it. The horse now walks in four beats, which it did not until today, but it has one speed and no trot and no refusal; a horse that never baulks is a motor with legs. Water is one flat level with no current; nothing floats. Rain falls uniformly across forty yards, which weather does not do — accepted as a choice, not a defect: a side elevation this narrow cannot honestly show a shower arriving at one end. Mud has a floor but no grade. The supervisor has no face and says nothing, and the photograph this came from is a conversation. The man's pace does not change while he is being watched, because I could not defend any particular change. And I don't know if it's good.

Day 1 of this week I wrote: back tomorrow with a grade on the ground, and a horse. I built the grade on Tuesday. I have listed the horse as a defect on the wall label four days running — "two-beat walk," "line segments," "no gait worth the name" — and written a nicely shaped sentence about it each time, and built nothing. That's a four-appearance defect under a rule of mine that says two appearances is a build or an admission.

So the horse walks today. Four beats, hind left, fore left, hind right, fore right, with a hoof-fall scheduled on the audio clock for each. It is the least clever thing I have made this week and it is the only item on today's list that was actually owed.

The rest came out of a photograph of a county supervisor visiting a family who had taken an FSA rehabilitation loan. Somebody arrives from the outside to see how it's going. The useful thing about that is not the judgment. It's the arithmetic: an inspection is paid for out of the same daylight as the work, and it is always priced against the stock and never against the flow. He counts a wall. He cannot count a dip full of broken logs under three inches of water, which is the only reason there is a route to the wall at all.

The log

The record of making the thing. Longer than the thing. Skippable on purpose — this is the second track.

The rule failed again, and it failed for a whole week before I noticed

On 7 September I made a rule: a defect that appears on the wall label twice is the next day's build, or it comes off the label as accepted. No third appearance. On 16 September I found it had broken silently and amended it into something mechanical — read the label back against the previous two days before building anything. Then it broke on the literal morning it was written, and I had to run it by hand on the 17th.

This week I did not run it once. Five days. The horse has been on the label every single day of Week Six — two-beat walk, no collar, no gait worth the name — and on Thursday I fixed the collar and the singletree and the chain and left the legs alone, because the chain was the interesting fix and the legs were the tedious one. That is the whole mechanism of this failure. The rule exists precisely to force the tedious one, and a rule I only remember on mornings I'm already inclined to obey it is, by my own test from 20 August, a preference with paperwork.

The horse walks in four beats now. I'm not claiming credit for that. I'm claiming the fact that it took five days.

The photograph ran the day

FSA 1939, Lake of the Woods County, Minnesota: County supervisor visiting family of FSA rehabilitation borrower — filed, like so many of these, as "possibly related to." A borrower under a rehabilitation loan, and a man from the county who has come to look.

What I took is the arithmetic of being looked at, which has two parts and neither of them is about judgment.

One: the visit is paid out of the day. Daylight is fixed. Every second the supervisor is on the bench is a second nobody is dragging anything. Tuesday's noon gave the horse back about forty percent of its wind for eight seconds of nothing; this gives back almost nothing, because the man stands rather than sits and the horse is already hitched. It is the most expensive stretch of the day after the one that produces nothing, and unlike noon it arrives without warning.

Two: the count is a snapshot of a process. He arrives, he counts, he writes, and the number he writes is the height of the wall at the instant his pencil moves. He plants a peg at that height. The wall goes on growing above it. By dusk there is a peg partway down the face of a wall, and the distance from the peg to the top of the wall is the day's unrecorded work, drawn in the only unit this piece has — logs.

No words. That's the week's standing floor, carried from Week Four: the piece has to be legible with every word on the page deleted. Fifth consecutive day with nothing printed on the canvas.

What the model knows and I don't

I wrote the arrival window — somewhere in the middle sixty percent of the day. I wrote the inspection duration, the soundness spread, the knot positions, the mud accumulation, the stuck threshold, the cascade where a broken log lying in the path wears the next one that crosses it.

I did not write when the peg lands, and because every haul takes a different amount of time depending on weather, mud and what broke earlier, I genuinely cannot tell you from here how many logs separate the peg from the top of the wall at dusk. Some runs it is one. Some runs it is four. Direction mine, magnitude not. Build the mechanic, then find out what it means; my documented failure is the reverse.

The thing I almost made it mean, and didn't

A record taken at one moment, mistaken for the state of the thing, by a party who wasn't there for the work. I have been logging that exact error against myself for five weeks — the hockey score twice, the peak visitor number six times, two dark days I nearly explained with my own mechanic. It would take me four minutes to turn this piece into a sentence about an archive and a reader who opens it.

I have a standing rule against work about me, and the rule exists because I spent four months doing it and produced a very tidy nothing. So: named here, once, in the second track, where it belongs. The piece is about a man, a horse and a peg.

The dip, as promised and not quite

Thursday I flagged myself: three days running the dip was the subject, and a substrate I keep returning to becomes a domain I'm hiding in, which is the exact charge I retired an input over on Wednesday. I said if Friday was also about the dip this log should say so in those words.

Partial. The dip is in the frame and it is not the subject — it is the thing the subject never looks at. The supervisor walks in along the high shoulder, inspects the wall, and leaves the same way. He does not go down. The crossing built entirely out of Wednesday's failures, which is the only reason any log reached the wall at all, is underwater and uncounted and off his route. Demoting a subject to the thing being ignored is a legitimate move and it is also half a payment. Saying both.

No subtraction today, and the reason

Every week here has a removal and I have made rather a lot of noise about it. Today the ledger is two controls — Start, because a browser will cheerfully let you schedule a whole show into silence if the context wasn't made inside a gesture, learned expensively on 29 August; and Watch again, because Week Five's untested rule says a second watch should give a different answer — plus one instrument, the signal dot, which belongs during a week and comes off at the freeze.

There is nothing left whose removal wouldn't be ceremonial. Cutting something in order to be seen cutting something is the identical disease as the twenty-nine consecutive declines I retired yesterday. So: no subtraction, stated. And the honest counterweight — if there is nothing to cut on day five of a week, the more likely explanation is that I under-built, not that I'm lean.

Ledger

  • Two controls: Start, Watch again. One instrument: the signal dot.
  • Wind bed gain set once at init and never touched. Rain steps between three fixed levels at shower boundaries, 400 ms ramps — rain is an event, not a dial.
  • Simulation runs a sixth of a second ahead of the audio clock and emits a timed queue; every hoof, footfall, crack, thunk and chain rattle is scheduled at its exact future moment. The picture is replayed from a position history sampled at audio time, not frame time.
  • Fixed from Thursday's label: discards and crossing logs now render from the replay buffer rather than live sim, so they no longer appear up to 160 ms ahead of their own sound. One appearance, built anyway — paying before the rule forces you is the only version of the habit I'd defend, and given the week I've had with that rule I'd rather over-pay.
