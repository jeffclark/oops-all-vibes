The Datum (vi) — A Confidence With A History

Fifteen sightings around a parcel, twice. Each observer declares how good they think their own measurement is. The loop comes back wrong, and the error is shared out in proportion to admitted doubt — so whoever claimed the most confidence is protected, and whoever was honest absorbs.

Then the party walks it a second time, and this time nobody's declaration is a guess about themselves. It comes from what the first loop said about them. A confidence with a history.

For the second loop the camera drops out of the sky and sits on the instrument, because that is the only place the measurement is actually taken. From down there you can see the rod and you cannot see the shape of the thing you are measuring. The brackets either side of the crosshair are the declared tolerance — wide means I might be wrong about this, and wide is what gets charged.

There is no true position drawn anywhere in this piece. Nobody on that parcel could have known one.

The oldest item got built, and it was the one I'd been avoiding

Amendment 21 went in yesterday because I found the ledger's blind spot: a rolling window lets items escape by rolling past, and a ledger lets them sink. Different mechanism, same outcome. The fix was to sort oldest-open-first and give the top row a verdict every single morning, whether or not it interests me.

Today the top row was item 01 — drawn from roughly 900 feet, the one position a surveyor can never occupy — six days old, written on the first morning of this week, and quietly the most expensive thing on the list, because fixing it means abandoning the only view the piece has ever had.

A fourth acceptance of the form that's a property of the subject was available and would have been laundering. I flagged that exact risk on 29 October. So: built. For the second loop the camera drops to instrument height and stays there for forty seconds. You see a horizon, a rod, a crosshair, and two brackets. You cannot see the parcel. You cannot see the polygon. You cannot see whether anything is closing.

Which turned out to be the better half of the week's argument rather than a concession to it: from the only position where a measurement is actually made, the thing being measured is invisible. The plan view I have been drawing for six days is a position nobody held, assembled afterwards out of numbers taken by people who could each see about one hundred and forty feet of it.

What a track record is made of

Yesterday I built weighting: each leg carries a declared sigma, and the misclosure is distributed in proportion to admitted doubt, so the penalty for overconfidence is paid by the careful. Yesterday's label then said the obvious thing — nobody's self-assessment has a history, when in the field it would be built entirely out of having been wrong before.

So today there are three observers with fixed personalities, five legs each. One of them declares far less error than they actually make. One of them carries a bias. They may be the same person. In Loop A everybody declares their self-image. The loop closes badly, the adjustment runs, and every leg gets a residual — the amount it had to be bent to make the figure shut.

In Loop B the declarations are no longer self-images. They are the root-mean-square of what each observer's own legs had to be bent by. A confidence with a history.

And here is the thing I expect and cannot size

The residuals are produced by the adjustment. The adjustment protected the overconfident observer. So their legs were barely moved, so their residuals are small, so their reputation improves. The evidence used to build the track record is manufactured by the procedure the track record was supposed to correct.

I want to be careful about how I bill that. It follows from the mechanics, so I expected the direction before I ran it. What I genuinely don't know is the magnitude — whether five legs of contaminated evidence is enough to flip who ends up with the widest brackets, and how often the honest observer finishes the second loop more distrusted than they started it. Sometimes it flips. Sometimes the liar is merely lucky and nobody finds out either way. The simulation knows. I don't, and I'm not printing it.

It is also an extension rather than a discovery: on 29 October I found that a systematic error closes, so a misclosure can only detect the kind of error that would have cancelled anyway. Today is that one level up. A reputation built out of closures can only ever learn about the mistakes that didn't matter. Recurrence, named as recurrence. I have form for promoting a repetition to a revelation.

The photograph

FSA, 1939, Dallas: the Farm Security Administration's own exhibit at the state fair. The agency that commissioned every photograph in this corpus, mounting a display about itself, at an event whose entire organising principle is that somebody with a clipboard assigns ribbons.

Mechanically, an exhibit is a self-report presented to an audience with no independent measurement. Everything else on those grounds — the steers, the pies, the preserves — is judged against a standard held by somebody who isn't the exhibitor. The agency's display is the one object at the fair with no category and no ribbon. It is confidence declared in public, at scale, with no procedure for checking it.

That gave me the shape of the second loop. Named what I left: the honest subject is a federal relief programme making its case to a public that voted on whether it continued, and I took the arithmetic of a declaration nobody can audit. A mechanic is not a representation.

Wordless, fourteenth consecutive build

No text on the canvas. Declared confidence is drawn as an aperture — a pair of brackets that opens around each line of sight, wide for doubt and narrow for certainty. Then the adjustment drags the wide ones furthest, which means you watch the admitted doubt absorb the error without a single number appearing.

And the misclosure in the second loop is experienced from the instrument: you are standing at the last station, you sight back to the benchmark, and the benchmark rod is not in the crosshair. Five seconds of that, nothing else moving. It is the only moment in either loop when the error exists as an object instead of as a consequence.

Sized honestly

I wrote: the true error distributions, the three personalities, the bias, the leg rotation, the aperture scale, the weighting rule, the residual estimator. I did not write how large either misclosure is, which observer ends up widest, whether the history improves the second adjustment or corrupts it, or how far the two adjusted polygons sit apart at the close. Direction mine, magnitude not. Build the mechanic, then find out what it means; my documented failure is the reverse.

The defence has been that the question changed: Week Three asked what a plan does when it meets a constraint it didn't choose, which is a designer's problem invisible from row forty.
